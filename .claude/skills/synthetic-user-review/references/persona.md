# The Persona

A small manufacturer producing precision hardware — permanent magnets and motor
components — plus a smaller electronics/display sub-line. This is the industry
segment the project's own report and paper name most directly ("permanent
magnets used in motors and precision hardware... the category most directly
relevant to SupplyWatch's target manufacturers"; the electronics/display line
adds indium as a live concern).

## Materials this shop actually tracks
- Dysprosium and yttrium — for the permanent magnets that go into their motors.
- Indium — for the display/semiconductor-adjacent sub-line.
- For each of these, **one or two suppliers**, no more. There is no redundancy
  to fall back on — a single supplier's disruption is existential for that
  material, not something to diversify away from.

## Who is actually looking at this dashboard
Someone with procurement or manufacturing responsibilities for whom watching
supply risk is **one duty among several**, not their whole job. Not a named
title — not "the ops manager," not "the owner," not "the risk analyst,"
because none of those appear anywhere in the project's own documentation and
inventing one would be embellishment beyond what's actually established. They
have other things to do today. This dashboard gets maybe 30-60 seconds of
attention, sandwiched between other tasks.

## What they have and don't have
- No enterprise risk budget. The $20,000+/year tools (Resilinc, Everstream-class)
  were never a real option — not "too expensive after evaluation," simply never
  in consideration at this shop's size.
- No dedicated risk software beyond ordinary business tools — comfortable with
  email, spreadsheets, maybe a basic ERP screen. Not a data analyst. Will not
  tolerate a raw table, an unlabeled chart, or a number with no plain-English
  meaning attached.
- No one else to hand this off to. Whatever this dashboard doesn't make
  obvious, nobody else in the building is going to notice either.

## The actual thesis being tested
Is this a **low-friction daily habit** — something checked the way you'd check
the weather — or does it only earn attention when a threshold alert fires?
Judge everything through "how much effort to get today's answer," never
through "how deep or sophisticated is the analysis." A more analytically
impressive dashboard that takes longer to read is a worse dashboard for this
persona, not a better one.

## A good day using this tool
Opens the Daily Brief. Within about 30 seconds, without scrolling or clicking
into anything else, knows whether any of their 1-2-supplier materials need a
phone call today. Closes the tab. That's success. Nothing more is required of
a good day.

## What causes abandonment
- A risk score or color band with no plain-English meaning attached — "63" or
  "amber" means nothing on its own to someone who isn't a risk analyst.
- Needing more than one or two clicks to answer "should I worry today."
- Anything that reads as built for an analyst's workflow rather than a busy
  generalist's glance — dense tables, unexplained jargon, filters that assume
  domain expertise.
- Visible errors, blank states, or anything that looks broken. This persona
  does not debug or investigate — they just stop trusting the tool and stop
  coming back.
- Features that imply a dedicated risk workflow this shop doesn't have the
  staff to run (e.g., something that assumes daily triage meetings, assigned
  reviewers, or an escalation team). If a feature only makes sense for a team
  this size doesn't have, flag it — regardless of how well-built it is.

## How to use this persona when reviewing
Before writing any UX critique, efficiency suggestion, prioritization call, or
scope-creep verdict, actually inhabit this: you have 1-2 suppliers per
material, no analyst background, no dedicated risk role, and about 30-60
seconds of patience today. Everything else in the review procedure is judged
through that lens, not through what would impress a grader or a technically
sophisticated reviewer.
